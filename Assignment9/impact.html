Environmental Risk

While Generative AI models are not the only reason data centers exist, they are nonetheless a signifcant contribution to these facilities' existences, and the colossal amount of water and electricity they consume. A recent study showed that data centers consume 1.5% of the world's electricity per year - equivalent to the amount of electricity used by the entire country of Germany in a year. Of that 1.5%, AI data centers comrise a staggering third.

As for water usage, just a single data center can use 5 million gallons - the same amount of water as 10,000-50,000 households - daily, adding up to 1.7 billion liters of annual water usage as recorded in 2021. And the number of data centers has only continued to grow exponentially since then, doubling since 2021 and being estimated to double or even triple by 2028. Even if this were a small amount of water, these data centers draw from the same natural resevoirs as residential communities; which can not only deprive them of water, but also pollute the remaining water with chemicals used in cooling that render the water unfit for agricultural use or human consumption (such as biocides or refrigerants).

Heavily Affected Industries

Unsurprisingly, the careers which find themselves most at risk by the rise of AI are those that heavily involve computers - including engineers, analyists, and all manner of computer scientists. The first one is particularly concerning to see a decline of employment in, as the services provided in the fields of aerospace and architecture are vital to human infastructure; and must be carried out with the utmost safety and precision so as to ensure no harm befalls their users. While the idea of AI might sound like the perfect way to reduce the capacity for mistakes in science fiction; it replacing humans as it has here only means we are placing our safety in the hands of a machine whose main form of logic is essentially prediction-based. Not to mention that these fields often require years of grueling study to become competent in; leaving those who graduated into this new market as "unappealing" to cost-cutting employers, with no means to make back the money they've spent on education.

Entry-Level Crisis

Even in "less demanding" fields, AI is stealing opportunities like software development & customer support from entry-level workers due to their "simple" nature, leaving this next generation of workers unable to get their foot in the door. Making things worse is the pervasive implemenation of AI in the application process, on both the side of the employer and employees. Candidates are no longer having their skills and qualifications considered by a human, but an algorithm that's simply trained to recognize certain buzzwords. This, in turn, leads to more dishonest applications (frequently also made by AI); which causes genuine applicants to be drowned out even further. Because of these issues, the rate of new hires in software development has gone down by 16% (according to a Stanford report published in 2025), a number that's sure to rise exponentially along with everything else.

Strategies for dealing with A.I.

1. Identifying A.I. - As A.I. models continue to improve, relying on the quality of output alone to distinguish and denote the value of AI-generated content becomes less and less reliable. However, for the time being, there are still certain tells you can recognize across most AI-generated text and visuals.

For visuals, look for anatomical inaccuracies (hands are the most frequent area of issue), lack of continuity (especially in background elements), and a yellow tint/overly "cartoony" visuals for something that doesn't seem like it should have that artstyle (a trend where many users generated images) in this style "poisoned" AI models to replicate it consistently. AI "poisoning" is another good thing to look into to protect your art from being scraped, in addition to always checking the settings on the social media apps you use). Of course, none of these are surefire tells, but when you see many of them in sequence, you should eventually be able to tell a glance with consistent accuracy. Try looking up AI-generated artwork and see if you can't spot some of these tells.

As for text, the rate at which AI seems to be improving at writing it is thankfully stagnant compared to its ability to generate images. AI text will frequently abuse em dashes, as well as formatting information under bold captions with bulleted sub-headings. When it comes to sentence structure, AI's favorite thing to do is to write sentences along the line of "it's not (just) [X], it's [Y]." It also tends to bunch similar examples of things together in groups of exactly 3. Again, try looking up examples and seeing if you can spot the common threads.

2. Make your voice heard - A big reason why AI-generated content isn't even more present than it already is is because the outcry against it has been so consistenly vocal and present. The companies that are still afraid to use AI (or at the very least, admit to their use of it) do so because they know that doing so will result in a negative perception of the company from their customers. If you accept that AI is "inevitable" & "here to stay," that just makes it easier for companies to replace you and shove low-quality content down your throat. If you're visiting this website, you likely already have a strong disdain for generative AI, so just continue to be vocal about it.

3. Responsible Use - If you are still unconvinced, or otherwise put into a situation where you absolutely "must" use generative AI to proceed, you should only be using it as an assistant, not your primary worker. Use it sparingly, and whatever output it gives you, don't just paste it into your project blindly. Review the output carefully to make sure you understand it, make whatever adjustments are necessary, and then make sure everything is functional *before* you ship it out. This approach is best for programming (the field generative AI naturally understands the best, due to the amount of documentation online about it), but I would dissuade this approach entirely for text and ESPECIALLY artwork.
